📈 Dairy Economic Outlook

Spring flush is here. Central Valley processors report milk output pushing against capacity limits. Spot Class III loads are available at $7-under to $2-under announced prices this week. Record U.S. dairy cow numbers (9.58 million head, up 189,000 year-on-year) and a 3.2% year-over-year production gain are keeping a ceiling on Class III.

Bottom line for producers: Margins are squeezed. Cost-of-production remains elevated. Herd culling decisions will be critical in 2026 for the long-term price recovery. Watch for late-year improvement if culling accelerates in Q1–Q2.

🏃 Demand & Export Bright Spots

Exports remain a bright spot. U.S. dairy exports in December 2025 hit 239,083 metric tons — up 15% year-over-year. Cheese exports up 24%; powder up 18%. Butter strong at 15,714 MT. The Middle East remains the leading cheese buyer.

Watch: New Zealand February milk production surged to record levels (+7.4% year-on-year), adding global supply competition. HPAI in dairy remains a risk factor for 2026 operations.

Crop / Sector April Weather Relevance & Action Items
🍐 Almonds / Pistachios Warming temps accelerating nut set. Peak water demand beginning. ET rates climbing rapidly — begin scheduling sustained drip runs. Warm nights support pest pressure (NOW, mites): scout weekly. No frost risk for these crops at this stage.
🍇 Grapes FROST ALERT mid-April nights. Bud break to 4-inch shoots — most vulnerable frost period. Have wind machines on standby. Minimum thermometers in every block. Warm daytime temps will push shoot growth fast; powdery mildew program should already be running at 0.5-inch.
🪓 Walnuts Warm April accelerates bud break — monitor for late frost events especially in low-lying ground through April 15. Codling moth degree-day accumulation is happening fast; traps should be deployed now. Walnut blight risk is lower with dry weather — a silver lining from the dry pattern.
🌾 Wheat / Small Grains Flag leaf to heading — warm, dry April weather is favorable for grain fill but increases stripe rust and aphid pressure. Check soil moisture at depth; April ET will outpace residual soil water quickly without irrigation. Final N top-dress window is closing fast.
🌿 Cotton / Field Crops Soil temps at 4-inch depth need to reach 65°F consistently. Kern and Kings county soils may reach that threshold by April 20–25 with this warm trend. Early planting into marginal temperatures risks stand failure. Wait for sustained warmth. Pre-plant irrigation 7–10 days before planting to firm beds.
🍊 Citrus Warm April drives bloom and early fruit set. Watch for citrus thrips as fruit sets. Moderate mild stress leading into bloom is beneficial — ease off irrigation at full petal drop. Asian Citrus Psyllid monitoring is a priority — report any suspicious yellowing or die-back to CDFA immediately.
30-Day Outlook (April 15 – May 15): Warm and dry conditions expected to persist through the balance of April and into May. Below-normal precipitation likelihood for the Central Valley. Temperatures trending 3–5°F above historical average. ET demand will ramp sharply — growers should be transitioning from soil-moisture storage reliance to active irrigation management now. Water availability constraints (see Section 5) make irrigation scheduling efficiency critical this season.

Section 5

Water Situation & District Updates

The 2026 water year is one of the most complex in recent memory. Reservoir storage remains above average thanks to prior wet years, but the near-total loss of the Sierra snowpack from March's record heat wave has fundamentally changed summer runoff projections. South-of-Delta agricultural allocations remain critically low.

🚫 Critical: Sierra Snowpack

DWR's April 1 snow survey at Phillips Station found no measurable snow — the second-lowest April reading on record. Statewide snowpack is now only 18% of average. The Northern Sierra, which feeds the state's largest water supply reservoirs, stands at just 6% of average.

The rapid melt was driven by a record-shattering late-March heat wave, combined with warm storms that delivered precipitation as rain rather than snow. Much of the snowpack was already gone well before the traditional April peak.

💧 Reservoir Storage: Silver Lining

Despite the snowpack crisis, major reservoir storage remains above average for this time of year — a buffer from prior wet years and effective water management. As of late February, statewide reservoirs were at 122% of average storage.

The critical question now is how long reservoir storage lasts through a long, hot, dry summer without significant snowmelt replenishment. Groundwater pumping will increase — a direct cost and long-term sustainability concern.

Madera Canal: Running in conjunction with Friant Class 1 deliveries. Check Madera Irrigation District's schedule for April turn schedules.

Delta-Mendota Canal (DMC): Limited deliveries reflecting south-of-Delta 20% allocation. Confirm actual delivery schedules with your water district and plan groundwater pumping accordingly.

Kings River / Tulare Lake Basin: Contact Consolidated Irrigation District, Kings River Conservation District, or your local water district for specific April delivery windows and turn schedules.

⚡ Key Water Management Priorities

With the snowpack effectively gone and summer-long reservoir drawdown ahead, irrigation efficiency is a financial and operational imperative this season:

• Irrigation scheduling efficiency: Use ET-based tools (CIMIS) to precisely match supply to crop demand. Avoid any waste.
• Groundwater strategy: Plan pumping now. Wells under increased demand valley-wide — coordinate with neighbors where possible.
• SGMA compliance: Increased groundwater use must be tracked per Sustainable Groundwater Management Act requirements.
• Drip & micro-efficiency: Every gallon counts. Check emitter function and distribution uniformity before peak demand.
• Crop prioritization: Growers with limited supply must prioritize water to highest-value perennial crops.
Water Advisory: The combination of near-zero snowpack and below-average CVP allocations for south-of-Delta contractors makes 2026 one of the most water-constrained seasons in recent memory despite above-average reservoir storage. Bureau of Reclamation has indicated it will continue monitoring hydrology and may adjust allocations — but upward revisions appear unlikely given the snowpack collapse. Contact your local water district for the most current delivery schedules and allocation status.

This Month in American History

April 30, 1789 — George Washington Takes the Oath of Office

On April 30, 1789, George Washington was inaugurated as the first President of the United States on the balcony of Federal Hall in New York City — then the nation’s capital. Standing before a crowd of thousands, he placed his hand on a Bible and solemnly swore to “faithfully execute the Office of President of the United States” — words that every president has repeated for more than 235 years since.

Washington had led the Continental Army through eight brutal years of war to win independence. He could have been made a king — many urged him to be — but he refused, choosing instead to serve as a president bound by a Constitution and to step aside voluntarily after two terms. In doing so, he set the most important democratic precedent in American history: that power belongs to the people, not the man.

His first inaugural address called upon “the sacred fire of liberty” entrusted to the American people — a flame kept burning in every generation by the men and women who chose to serve and protect it.

The preservation of the sacred fire of liberty and the destiny of the republican model of government are justly considered as deeply, perhaps as finally, staked on the experiment entrusted to the hands of the American people.

— George Washington
