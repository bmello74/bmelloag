📈 Dairy Economic Outlook

More milk, again. The August 12 WASDE raised 2026 U.S. production to 238.1 billion lbs — up 6.4 billion from 2025 — on higher cow numbers. For 2026 the Class III forecast rose to $16.25 on firmer cheese and whey, while Class IV was cut to $18.15 on softer butter and powder.

Watch the depooling risk. July marked the sixth consecutive month Class IV finished above Class III. When that gap holds, handlers have an incentive to depool the higher-value milk, which can pull down the blend price and the producer price differential you actually get paid on. Check your PPD line, not just the announced class price.

Bottom line: Cheaper corn (Section 2) is the one lever moving your way. Feed cost discipline and component focus are what protect margin into the fall.

🏃 The Convergence & Export Story

An unusual alignment. Spot cheese, butter, and milk powder are all trading neck and neck in the $1.50s — a convergence you rarely see. It reflects a market that has found a floor rather than one still falling, and week-to-week moves have been modest.

Exports are doing the heavy lifting. Cheese shipments to Mexico hit a fresh record in June, and butter and milkfat exports ran 48 percent above a year earlier. Those shipments are the reason product prices have held in the $1.50s instead of sliding further.

The catch: that export pace has to hold. Prices must stay low enough to keep the shipments moving — if exports slow to a walk, the cheese backs up here.

⚠ What’s Driving CA Prices

Two forces are working against you at the pump this month:

• Crude turned back up. September WTI is near $81/barrel, up roughly $10 from July. Every $10/barrel is worth about 24¢/gal at the pump.
• Refinery closures removed roughly 18–21 percent of in-state refining capacity — a lasting squeeze that amplifies every crude move here.
• CARB programs: Cap-and-Invest plus the LCFS update effective July 1, 2026 are now fully in the price.
• Taxes: California’s roughly $0.90/gal remains the highest in the nation.

📊 30-Day Fuel Price Outlook

Crude has given back some ground in the last few sessions, but the July lows are gone. Expect Central Valley clear diesel to hold in the $6.40–$6.80/gal range through harvest, with red diesel roughly 90¢ under that.

What would change it: a sustained pullback in crude would filter through in two to three weeks. A Black Sea or Middle East supply event would push the other way, and California's thin refining margin means moves land here harder than anywhere else.

Action: If you passed on locking bulk diesel in July, the cheap window has closed for now. Price harvest and hauling on $6.60–$6.80 rather than the July number, and revisit contracts if crude breaks back under $75.
Crude Oil Context. September WTI settled at $81.25/barrel on August 13, down $2.02 on the day but roughly $10 above where it traded in early July. That round trip is the whole fuel story this month: the relief we flagged in July reversed, and California pump prices followed within weeks. Crop / Sector August Weather Relevance & Action Items
🍐 Almonds / Pistachios Almond harvest is on, roughly one to three weeks early after the warm spring. Shake into the cool window, and use the below-normal highs and dry air for clean windrow drying. Time your pre-harvest irrigation cutoff carefully — too wet and you fight mud and shaker damage, too dry and you stress trees into next season. Pistachio harvest started this week — also ahead of the normal late-August to September window, on a light crop: an off year in the alternate-bearing cycle, made worse by the unusually warm March that disrupted bloom. Two harvests running at once means equipment, crews, and hauling are all competing. Keep post-harvest irrigation planned now so trees are not left dry once shaking moves on — on a light year that recovery window matters even more for setting 2027.
🍇 Grapes Harvest is underway for early varieties and raisin lay-down is approaching. Cooler days slow sugar accumulation slightly but protect against raisining and sunburn — check Brix more often than the calendar suggests. Dry weather is ideal for raisin drying on trays. Keep the mildew program tight right through the last pick.
🪓 Walnuts Kernel fill and hull development heading toward a September harvest. Stay on husk fly and watch the third codling moth flight on degree-day accumulation. Do not back off irrigation yet — water stress now costs kernel weight and drives shrivel. The cooler stretch reduces sunburn risk on exposed nuts.
🌾 Wheat / Small Grains Ground is open and this is the prime amendment window before fall planting. Get soil and water samples pulled now so there is time to act on the results before planting. Correcting salinity, sodium, and infiltration is far cheaper on bare ground than mid-season — and if the winter comes in wet, that moisture will incorporate your amendments for free.
🌿 Cotton / Field Crops Boll fill and cutout in Kern and Kings county fields. The cooler stretch reduces shed risk, but keep water ahead of demand through fill — late stress costs micronaire and turnout. With December cotton up near 83¢, this crop is worth finishing properly. Keep scouting lygus and mites until cutout is confirmed.
🍊 Citrus Fruit is sizing and the cooler window eases sunburn and splitting pressure. Keep irrigation frequent and even — swings between wet and dry are what crack fruit. Continue Asian Citrus Psyllid monitoring and report any suspicious yellowing or die-back to CDFA immediately.
30-Day Outlook (Aug 15 – Sep 15): Expect the current cool stretch to give way to renewed heat in the back half of August — long-range guidance has consistently pointed to a hot mid-to-late August — then a gradual seasonal cooling into September. Rainfall stays at essentially zero. ET demand eases slightly off its July peak but remains high, so keep matching applications to CIMIS demand rather than to the calendar.

Looking further out — and this one has a deadline attached: the Climate Prediction Center has El Niño strengthening through the rest of 2026, with above-normal precipitation favored across much of the southern U.S. this fall and winter. That is a meaningful change from the last two winters. It is early, and El Niño signals are less reliable for Central California than for the south state — but if it verifies, the stretch between harvest and unworkable ground gets short. Water Situation & District Updates

Good news, three days old. On August 11 Reclamation raised south-of-Delta CVP allocations again — irrigation contractors from 25% to 28%, and south-of-Delta M&I from 75% to 78%. It is the third increase this year and reflects better-than-expected supply conditions plus added operational flexibility. It is still a short year, but the direction has been consistently upward since the 15% initial allocation in February.

✅ Allocation Raised Again — Aug 11

South-of-Delta irrigation contractors moved from 25% to 28% of contract total, and south-of-Delta M&I from 75% to 78% of historic use or public health and safety needs, whichever is greater. All other amounts announced May 19 are unchanged.

Track the year: 15% initial in February, 20% in March, 25% in May, and now 28%. Reclamation cited improved supply conditions and added operational flexibility. If you budgeted on 25%, there is a little more water available than you planned for — confirm the delivery timing with your district before you commit it.

💧 Storage & the Drawdown Question

The snowpack has been gone since early summer, so everything from here runs on stored water. Storage held up well enough through spring capture to support two allocation increases, and Folsom was still running above its long-term average for the date in early August.

The question for the rest of the season is drawdown pace. Reclamation is also managing reservoir releases against downstream temperature requirements, which constrains how freely water moves. Expect groundwater pumping to stay heavy through harvest — a direct energy cost at $6.62 diesel, and an ongoing SGMA-compliance concern.

⚡ Key Water Management Priorities

With harvest underway and the season's water nearly committed, the priorities shift from rationing to placement — getting what you have where it does the most good:

• Schedule the new 28%. The incremental allocation only helps if it is on the delivery calendar. Call your district this week.
• Infiltration, not just volume. Hot, dry ground repels water. If you are seeing pooling, runoff, or dry pockets under emitters, you are losing water you already paid for (see Section 6).
• Distribution uniformity: Check emitter function and pressure now — a clogged block wastes allocation and stresses trees at the same time.
• SGMA compliance: Heavy pumping still has to be tracked and reported per Sustainable Groundwater Management Act requirements.
• Post-harvest planning: Trees need water after shaking. Do not let the allocation run dry before post-harvest irrigation, which sets next year's crop.

🌰 2026 Nut Harvest Watch

A smaller crop, an early harvest, and firmer prices. USDA’s forecast puts the 2026 California almond crop at 2.7 billion lbs, down 1% from last year, with yield holding at 1,940 lbs per acre. Note that this is the only official NASS estimate this year — the mid-season Objective Measurement was discontinued, so there will be no recalibration before harvest wraps. Bearing acreage also fell to roughly 1.386 million acres, the first recorded decrease since 1995.

Warm spring weather pushed development ahead, and harvest is running one to three weeks early across many areas. Prices firmed through July on strong export demand — June shipments up 14% year over year, current-crop sales up 48%, with India buying hard ahead of Diwali. Pistachio harvest started this week — early again, against a normal late-August to September start. The crop is light, and it is light for two reasons stacked on top of each other: 2026 is a natural off year in the alternate-bearing cycle, and an unusually warm March compounded it, disrupting bloom timing and the male-to-female overlap that pollination depends on. An off year alone trims a crop. An off year plus a warm spring is what takes it down to grower estimates near 600–800 million lbs. With both nut crops shaking at once, the squeeze is on hauling, huller capacity, and crews.

What that means for you: a smaller crop into a firmer market rewards finishing well. Protect kernel quality through harvest, and do not shortchange post-harvest irrigation and soil correction — that window is what sets the 2027 crop.

And it moves your calendar up. Both crops running early means the post-harvest window opens earlier than usual — but it does not push the rain back. If anything, an early harvest into a potentially wet fall is the best argument there is for getting the sampling booked now rather than after the last load leaves.

This Month in American History

August 14, 1945 — V-J Day and the End of the Second World War

Eighty-one years ago yesterday — on August 14, 1945 — President Truman stepped before reporters and announced that Japan had accepted the terms of surrender. The Second World War was over. Word moved across the country in minutes: church bells, factory whistles, car horns, and strangers pouring into the streets of towns that had spent four years reading casualty lists.

More than 16 million Americans had served in uniform. More than 400,000 did not come home. Behind them stood a home front that had rationed, rebuilt, and retooled — including the farmers of this valley, who were told plainly that food was a weapon and who answered by growing more of it, with fewer hands, than anyone thought possible.

The men who came back put down their rifles and picked up shovels. They planted the orchards, dug the wells, and built the packing sheds that a great many Central Valley operations still run on today. When we talk about generational farms here, we are often talking about ground first worked by someone who had just come home from a war.
