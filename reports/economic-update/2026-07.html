Central Valley plants are running full, and summer heat is beginning to pressure component levels even as demand stays firm.

📈 Dairy Economic Outlook

Supply keeps growing. The July WASDE raised 2026 U.S. milk production to 236.6 billion lbs and lifted cow inventories again. That steady growth is what pulled Class III, Class IV, and the all-milk forecast lower this month. Central Valley plants are running near capacity through the summer.

Bottom line for producers: Margins are tight and getting tighter as milk checks ease. Watch feed costs — cheaper corn (see Section 2) is the main relief valve. Disciplined culling and component focus will protect margins into the fall.

🏃 Demand & Export Bright Spots

Exports are the bright spot. The July WASDE raised 2026 export forecasts on a skim-solids basis, driven by strong cheese and whey-containing shipments. New U.S. cheese capacity — including Land O’Lakes’ announced Tulare protein expansion — is helping move the growing milk supply into higher-value products.

Watch: Imports of butter and milk proteins were also raised, and global powder supply remains ample. HPAI in dairy herds remains a background risk for 2026 operations.

Fuel Prices & Outlook

California fuel prices have eased meaningfully from their spring records as crude has fallen back to the low-$70s/barrel. Diesel is off its highs but still the priciest in the nation, and structural pressures — refinery closures, CARB program costs, and the nation’s highest fuel taxes — keep Central Valley ag fuel expensive heading into peak irrigation-pumping season.

⚠ What’s Driving CA Prices

Even after the spring pullback, California prices stay well above the U.S. average because of structural factors:

• Refinery closures removed roughly 18–21% of in-state refining capacity — a lasting supply squeeze.
• Crude relief: WTI back in the low-$70s has pulled diesel and gasoline off spring records.
• CARB programs: Cap-and-Invest plus the LCFS update that took effect July 1, 2026 add cost at the pump.
• Taxes: California’s ~$0.90/gal is still the highest in the nation.

📊 30-Day Fuel Price Outlook

The EIA expects Brent to average about $74/barrel in Q3 and slip toward $70 by Q4 2026 on building global inventories — a downward bias for diesel and gasoline barring a supply shock.

Central Valley diesel is likely to drift in the $5.75–$6.25/gal range through summer, with red diesel roughly $0.90 under that. The wildcard is the Strait of Hormuz: renewed Iran strikes could snap prices higher quickly.

Action: With crude soft, this is a reasonable window to lock a portion of your summer bulk diesel and fertilizer needs.
Crude Oil Context. WTI opened near $71.77/barrel on July 10 and climbed back above $74 by July 13 as the U.S. carried out a fourth strike on Iran and Tehran declared the Strait of Hormuz closed “until further notice.” Every $10/barrel move in crude is worth roughly $0.24/gal at the pump — so this remains the single biggest swing factor for your fuel budget.

Weather Outlook & Agricultural Relevance

Mid-July brings peak summer heat to the Central Valley, with Fresno-area highs running from the upper 90s into the 100s and an excessive-heat stretch that opened the month. Combined with the early loss of the Sierra snowpack, evapotranspiration (ET) demand is at its seasonal maximum — making precise, efficient irrigation the single most important management task on the ranch right now.

Crop / Sector July Weather Relevance & Action Items
🍐 Almonds / Pistachios Almond hull split is underway — time NOW and mite sprays to the split window. Pistachios are in nut fill / shell hardening, but this is an off year compounded by March heat, so the 2026 crop is light (see Product Spotlight). Peak ET now: run sustained irrigation and keep trees unstressed to protect next year’s buds.
🍇 Grapes Approaching veraison. Manage canopy for shade to limit sunburn on clusters, and hold an even irrigation program — deficit strategies for wine grapes, full supply for table and raisin. Keep the powdery mildew program tight as clusters close and harvest nears.
🪓 Walnuts Nut sizing and kernel fill. Watch the second flight of codling moth on degree-day accumulation and stay on husk fly. Sunburn risk is real on exposed nuts — maintain irrigation to prevent shrivel and hold kernel quality through the heat.
🌾 Wheat / Small Grains Harvest is complete. Focus shifts to residue management and ground prep for fall rotations. A dry, hot summer is the ideal window to apply soil amendments and correct pH or salinity issues before the next crop goes in.
🌿 Cotton / Field Crops Peak bloom and boll set in Kern and Kings county fields. Extreme heat can shed squares and young bolls — keep irrigation ahead of ET to avoid water stress during fruit set. Scout aggressively for lygus/plant bug and mites through the bloom period.
🍊 Citrus Fruit is sizing through the heat. Protect against sunburn (kaolin clay, adequate canopy) and keep irrigation frequent and even to prevent fruit drop and splitting. Continue Asian Citrus Psyllid monitoring — report suspicious yellowing or die-back to CDFA immediately.
30-Day Outlook (July 15 – Aug 15): Hot and dry conditions will dominate, with repeated excessive-heat episodes and effectively zero rainfall — typical peak-summer Valley weather. Temperatures are likely to run at or above the ~102°F seasonal average. ET demand is at its yearly maximum, so irrigation efficiency directly determines both yield and cost. Use CIMIS ET data to match applications to demand and avoid both stress and waste (see Section 5).

Section 5

Water Situation & District Updates

The 2026 water year has settled into a two-track story. The Sierra snowpack melted out early after the warm spring, so there is essentially no snowmelt left to feed summer runoff — yet major reservoirs remain at or above average storage after coordinated capture of spring flows. On the strength of that storage, Reclamation raised south-of-Delta CVP allocations to 25% in May, a modest but welcome improvement.

🚫 Snowpack: Gone for the Season

After an early-melting, well-below-average snowpack, the Sierra is now essentially bare for the summer. There is no meaningful snowmelt left to supplement river flows, so from here forward crop water comes from stored reservoir supply, project deliveries, and groundwater.

The early melt traces back to the warm spring and the record-shattering March heat wave that also cut short the pistachio bloom. It is a reminder that a single hot month can reshape the entire water year.

💧 Reservoir Storage: Silver Lining

The silver lining is real: major reservoirs remain at or above average for mid-summer after aggressive capture of spring runoff, with San Luis and other project facilities in solid shape. That storage is exactly what let Reclamation raise south-of-Delta allocations to 25%.

The question for the rest of the season is drawdown pace through a long, hot, dry summer with no snowmelt behind it. Expect groundwater pumping to climb — a direct energy cost and an ongoing SGMA-compliance concern.

Kings River / Tulare Lake Basin: Contact Consolidated Irrigation District, Kings River Conservation District, or your local district for current summer delivery windows and turn schedules.

⚡ Key Water Management Priorities

With the snowpack gone and reservoir drawdown now underway, irrigation efficiency is the financial and operational priority for the rest of the season:

• Irrigation scheduling efficiency: Use ET-based tools (CIMIS) to precisely match supply to crop demand. Avoid any waste.
• Groundwater strategy: Plan pumping now. Wells under increased demand valley-wide — coordinate with neighbors where possible.
• SGMA compliance: Increased groundwater use must be tracked per Sustainable Groundwater Management Act requirements.
• Drip & micro-efficiency: Every gallon counts. Check emitter function and distribution uniformity before peak demand.
• Crop prioritization: Growers with limited supply must prioritize water to highest-value perennial crops.
Water Advisory: Reclamation raised south-of-Delta CVP allocations to 25% in May on the strength of above-average reservoir storage, and Eastside contractors moved from 0 to 49,000 AF. Even so, with no snowpack behind summer flows, 2026 remains a supply-constrained year that will lean heavily on stored water and groundwater. Reclamation continues to monitor hydrology through summer. Contact your local water district for the most current delivery schedules and allocation status.

🍌 2026 Pistachio Crop Watch

A lighter crop this year — but a strong setup for 2027. California’s pistachios are in a natural off year of the alternate-bearing cycle, and a record-warm March hit right at bloom — a one-two punch that has trimmed yields, especially south of Fresno County. Some grower estimates put total receipts as low as 700–900 million lbs, potentially less than half of the record 2025 crop, and with Turkey down and Iran struggling, global supply is expected to be tight.

The good news, per Pacific Nut Producer: growers and processors view 2026 as an anomaly and expect an excellent 2027 yield. That makes proper soil amendments this off-year just as important as harvest — use the lighter crop load and the post-harvest window to correct soil moisture, salinity, and nutrient-holding capacity so trees enter next spring healthy and primed to set a heavy “on” crop.

This Month in American History

July 20, 1969 — Apollo 11 and the First Steps on the Moon

On July 20, 1969, the Apollo 11 lunar module Eagle touched down on the Sea of Tranquility, and Neil Armstrong became the first human being to set foot on the Moon — followed minutes later by Buzz Aldrin, while Michael Collins orbited overhead. An estimated 600 million people around the world watched or listened as an American flag was planted on another world.

The landing fulfilled the challenge President John F. Kennedy had issued in 1961 — to land a man on the Moon and return him safely before the decade was out. It was carried on the shoulders of roughly 400,000 American engineers, scientists, technicians, machinists, seamstresses, and factory workers whose ordinary, disciplined labor added up to something extraordinary.

The plaque the crew left behind reads: “Here men from the planet Earth first set foot upon the Moon, July 1969 A.D. We came in peace for all mankind.” It stands as a testament to American courage, ingenuity, and the willingness to attempt hard things — the same spirit that clears ground, plants orchards, and builds a life in the Central Valley.
