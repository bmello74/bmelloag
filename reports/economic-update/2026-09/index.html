📈 Dairy Economic Outlook

More milk, again. The September 11 WASDE raised the 2026 U.S. production forecast another 600 million lbs to 237.2 billion lbs, and raised 2027 by the same amount to 238.6 billion. The 2026 all-milk price came up a nickel to $19.90/cwt; Class III was trimmed to $16.20 and Class IV raised 40¢ to $18.55.

First real look at 2027. USDA has all-milk at $19.80, Class III at $17.10, and Class IV at $17.55, a flip, with Class III finally back on top next year. Useful context if you are pricing forward contracts this fall.

Bottom line: the cheap-feed tailwind that carried Valley dairies through summer is gone (Section 2), and the cheese side of your check got cheaper at the same time. That is the squeeze heading into Q4.

🏃 The Powder Rally & Depooling Watch

Powder is carrying the market. Nonfat dry milk at $1.995 is doing what cheese and butter are not. That props up Class IV while cheese blocks at $1.42 pull Class III down, which means the spread that narrowed to 72¢ in August is set to widen straight back out in September and October.

That is a depooling problem. When Class IV sits well above Class III, handlers have an incentive to pull the higher-value milk off the pool, and the blend price and producer price differential you actually get paid on move against you. Check the PPD line, not the announced class price.

What to watch: whether the powder rally holds through Q4, and whether export shipments keep clearing cheese. If exports slow, cheese backs up here and Class III has further to fall.

The cause is global this time: the Strait of Hormuz has been closed since late February, and a drone strike took Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline offline. This lands squarely on nut harvest, hauling, and every pump still running.

⚠ What’s Driving This

This one is not California’s doing. The pressure is global and it is landing on distillate hardest:

• Strait of Hormuz closed since late February, normally about 20% of world oil moves through it.
• Saudi East-West pipeline offline after a drone strike, taking roughly 4% of global supply out.
• Distillate inventories are tight and refining capacity has been damaged, diesel is being hit far harder than gasoline.
• Brent ran from $68.53 on July 2 to $109.51 on September 9.
• California on top of that: refinery closures, CARB diesel spec, roughly $0.90/gal in state taxes, and thin West Coast logistics.

📊 30-Day Fuel Price Outlook

As long as Hormuz stays closed there is no case for a return to a $6 handle. Plan harvest and hauling on $8.00–$8.40/gal clear and roughly $7.10–$7.50 red for the next 30 days, and treat anything below that as a break rather than the new normal.

The second wave is coming. Diesel rolls into freight, fertilizer, and custom rates on a six-to-ten week lag. The fall inputs you price in November will carry September’s fuel in them. Get quotes in writing now where you can.

Action: Talk to your fuel supplier about volume contracts this week. And if you do custom work, put a fuel surcharge line on the invoice rather than absorbing a $1.50/gal move, at these levels that is not a rounding error.
Crude Oil Context. WTI traded near $102.30/barrel and Brent near $106.50 on September 15, WTI up roughly 21% in a month and 58% year to date. Every $10/barrel move is worth about 24¢/gal at the pump, which is why a $30 crude move since July has produced a $1.50 diesel move here. California’s thin refining margin means every global swing lands harder in this state than anywhere else in the country.

Water Situation & District Updates

The 2026 water year closes September 30. South-of-Delta CVP contractors finish at 28%, raised four times from a 15% initial allocation in February. It was a short year that ended better than it started. Attention now turns to 2027, and for once the setup looks favorable: carryover storage is respectable at most major reservoirs and a strong El Niño is building. Two cautions, Pine Flat, the Kings River supply, is the weakest large reservoir in the state at 16% of capacity, and DWR sets December’s initial Table A allocation on storage, not on a forecast.

✅ Water Year 2026 Closes September 30

South-of-Delta irrigation contractors finish the year at 28% and south-of-Delta M&I at 78%. Track the year: 15% initial in February, 20% in March, 25% in May, 28% on August 11. Four increases off a very thin start.

Friant Division closes at 100% Class 1 with Class 2 at zero. Sacramento River Settlement contractors and north-of-Delta M&I finish at 100%.

Two things to do before September 30: reconcile actual deliveries against what you scheduled, and get any rescheduling request for unused allocation filed with your district. Water left unscheduled when the books close is water you paid for and did not get.

💧 Carryover Storage, and the Kings River Problem

Most of the major reservoirs enter the new water year in reasonable shape: Shasta 56% of capacity (96% of average), Oroville 54% (92%), San Luis 66% (168% of average), New Melones 66% (114%), Trinity 71% (111%).

Pine Flat is the outlier at 16% of capacity and 49% of average. The Kings River system drew down hard this summer. For Kings and Fresno county operations that is the number to watch into 2027, a low starting point means a conservative early entitlement no matter what the winter does.

Folsom at 46% of capacity (85% of average) is also running below par.

⚡ Key Water Management Priorities

The season shifts from rationing to closing out the year and setting up the next one:

• Close the water year properly. Reconcile deliveries and file rescheduling requests before September 30.
• Post-harvest irrigation is not optional. Trees set next year’s crop now. Do not let the allocation run dry before the last block gets water.
• Pumping just got expensive. At $8.14 diesel, every unnecessary pump hour costs materially more than it did in August. Check distribution uniformity before you run more water at a problem.
• Test before you amend. If you are planning gypsum, sulfur, zeolite, or a leaching run this fall, the soil and water report is what sets the rate.
• SGMA: heavy summer pumping still has to be tracked and reported.
• Plan for a wet winter. Clear tailwater ponds, ditches, and culverts before October rather than during the first storm.

🌰 2026 Nut Harvest Watch

Almonds: a smaller crop moving into a firm market. Nonpareil and Independence are essentially complete, with pollinizers still coming off. August receipts hit 401.9 million lbs, 55% above last year, almost entirely because harvest started two weeks early rather than because the crop is bigger. Pistachios are wrapping up a light off-year crop, the alternate-bearing down cycle compounded by the warm March that disrupted bloom overlap. Walnut harvest is just getting started; keep water on until you shake, then get post-harvest irrigation back on quickly.

What that means for you: a smaller crop into a firmer market rewards finishing well. Protect kernel quality through the last shake, and do not shortchange post-harvest irrigation or soil correction, that window is what sets the 2027 crop. And with harvest running early into a fall that may turn wet early, the post-harvest window opens sooner but does not necessarily last longer.

And do not skip post-harvest irrigation. At $8.14 diesel, pumping is genuinely expensive right now and it is tempting to shut down early. A tree that goes into dormancy dry gives you a lighter 2027, budget the water, and make it go further by fixing whatever the soil report says is actually wrong.

This Month in American History

September 17, 1787, The Signing of the Constitution

Two days from now, on September 17, the United States marks 239 years since thirty-nine delegates put their names to the Constitution in the Pennsylvania State House in Philadelphia. They had spent a hot summer behind closed windows arguing over how much power a government should have and how much it should be denied, and what they produced was not a perfect document. Several delegates refused to sign it. Those who did knew it would need amending.

What made it remarkable was not that it settled every question. It was that it created a way to keep answering them without tearing the country apart, and then handed that job to ordinary citizens. Farmers, tradesmen, shopkeepers. People with work to do and an interest in being left alone to do it.

That is still the arrangement. The right to own your ground, sign your own contracts, argue with your water district, and vote out the people setting the rules you farm under all traces back to four pages signed by thirty-nine men who were not certain it would work. Constitution Day is Thursday. It is worth the minute.

A republic, if you can keep it.

Benjamin Franklin, on leaving the Convention
