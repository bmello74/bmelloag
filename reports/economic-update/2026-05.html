📈 Dairy Economic Outlook

Spring flush is peaking. Central Valley processors continue to report milk output pushing capacity. Spot Class III loads remain available at $5-under to $3-under announced prices through mid-May. U.S. dairy cow numbers stand at 9.62 million head (up 213,000 year-on-year). Production gains tracking 2.9% above year-ago levels.

Heat stress watch: May daytime highs above 90°F now driving cow comfort concerns. Expect components (fat & protein) to soften in late May into June. Margins remain tight. Plan heat abatement, ration adjustments, and culling decisions carefully. Q2 culling will set the tone for late-2026 price recovery.

🏃 Demand & Export Bright Spots

Exports remain a key support. U.S. dairy exports in March 2026 hit 248,420 metric tons — up 12% year-over-year. Cheese exports up 19%; powder up 22%. Butter exports strong at 16,840 MT. The Middle East and Southeast Asia continue as leading buyers; Mexico cheese demand robust.

Watch: New Zealand production starting seasonal decline into autumn (Southern Hemisphere). May provides natural global tightening window. HPAI monitoring continues; CA dairy biosecurity protocols essential through summer.

⚠ Why Prices Are So High

California fuel price pressure continues in May 2026:

• Refinery maintenance season tightening in-state supply during peak demand transition.
• Summer fuel blend required May 1 — reformulated CARB gasoline is more costly to produce.
• CARB Cap-and-Invest currently adds ~$0.26/gal; LCFS update July 1, 2026 expected to add another $0.65/gal.
• California taxes add ~$0.92/gal — highest in the nation.
• Summer travel demand ramping up; Memorial Day weekend typical seasonal price peak.

📊 30-Day Fuel Price Outlook

Analyst base-case projects California regular gasoline averaging $6.10–$6.65/gal through summer, with diesel tracking in the $7.75–$8.25/gal range. July 1 LCFS update could push prices to upper bound.

Agricultural impacts: Nitrogen fertilizer prices have moderated as crude stabilized, with delivered Central Valley urea at $425–$485/ton — still well above pre-2025 levels.

Action: Lock in summer fuel contracts now. Peak irrigation demand will coincide with seasonally high prices. Consider bulk fuel storage if storage capacity available.
Crude Oil Context. WTI crude closed at $86.42/barrel on May 12 with the extended ceasefire holding. Every $10/barrel move in crude translates to approximately $0.24/gal at the pump. Despite crude moderation, California fuel prices remain elevated due to structural in-state costs. Monitor refinery maintenance schedules and the July 1 LCFS update which could add $0.65/gal.

Section 5

Water Situation & District Updates

May is the make-or-break month for 2026 Central Valley water management. With snowpack runoff largely consumed by early summer heat, surface water deliveries are well underway and groundwater is filling the gap. Operations must execute efficient irrigation now — the next 90 days will determine which farms protect yields and which see significant losses.

🚫 Critical: Runoff Status

May 1 DWR final snow survey confirmed statewide snowpack at just 24% of average, far below normal water-year benchmarks. Northern Sierra finished at 9% of average; Central Sierra at 28%; Southern Sierra at 35%. The early-season runoff peak has already passed in most river systems.

Compressed runoff means rivers are dropping quickly toward summer base flows. Water districts and Bureau of Reclamation are managing tight summer release schedules. Surface water deliveries are happening at higher rates than typical for May to take advantage of remaining runoff.

💧 Reservoir Storage Update

As of May 1, statewide reservoirs were at 112% of average storage — still above normal but drawing down rapidly. Shasta sits at 84% capacity; Oroville at 88%. The question now is drawdown rate through a long, hot, dry summer.

Bureau of Reclamation has reaffirmed CVP allocations published earlier in the year. Some south-of-Delta contractors may see updates as runoff data finalizes through May. Groundwater pumping demand is accelerating valley-wide — SGMA reporting requirements remain in full effect.

Kings River / Tulare Lake Basin: Run times extended to support late-spring crop demands. Contact Consolidated Irrigation District, Kings River Conservation District, or your local water district for current turn schedules.

⚡ Key Water Management Priorities

With peak heat already here and a long, dry summer ahead, irrigation efficiency is the difference between profit and loss this season:

• Solution-grade gypsum applications now: Improve infiltration, reduce surface crusting, and increase water-holding capacity — every irrigation event reaches the root zone more effectively.
• Irrigation scheduling efficiency: Use ET-based tools (CIMIS) to match daily supply to crop demand. No guesswork.
• Groundwater management: Coordinate with neighbors. Tank capacities tightening across the valley.
• SGMA compliance: Track every acre-foot pumped. Increased reliance on groundwater means accurate reporting is non-negotiable.
• Crop prioritization: Limited supply growers must direct water to highest-value perennials first.
Water Advisory: The combination of poor snowpack runoff, accelerated reservoir drawdown, and below-average CVP south-of-Delta allocations makes May–September 2026 one of the most water-constrained periods in recent memory. Bureau of Reclamation continues monitoring conditions — some upward revisions for SWP are still possible but CVP increases unlikely. Soil amendments that improve infiltration and water retention are now critical tools, not optional upgrades. Contact your local water district for the most current delivery schedules and allocation status.

This Month in American History

Memorial Day — Honoring Those Who Gave All

On Monday, May 25, 2026, the United States will pause to observe Memorial Day — a sacred day set aside to honor the men and women of the Armed Forces who gave their lives in service to our nation. It is not a celebration of military service in general — that is Veterans Day in November — but a solemn remembrance of those who never came home.

The tradition traces back to the years after the Civil War, when communities across the country — both North and South — began decorating the graves of fallen soldiers with flowers and flags. Originally called Decoration Day, the observance grew into a national tradition. In 1971, Congress formally established Memorial Day as a federal holiday on the last Monday in May.

From the battlefields of Gettysburg to the beaches of Normandy, from the jungles of Vietnam to the deserts of Iraq and Afghanistan, more than 1.3 million American service members have died defending the freedoms we exercise every day. Today, in fields across the Central Valley, we plant and harvest in peace because of them.
