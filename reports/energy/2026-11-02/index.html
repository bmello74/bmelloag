📈  This Week’s Call

Diesel fell 9.9 cents and wholesale gave back its whole jump. We called it higher and missed again, so we are moving to flat to lower.

Last Monday we called California diesel modestly higher at $8.42 to $8.62 and put the odds of a new record near 40%. That call missed for the second week running. AAA’s Sunday read puts diesel at $8.3562, down 9.9 cents on the week, 6.4 cents below the bottom of our range and 15.6 cents under the $8.5123 record of October 18. We had the direction wrong again. EIA’s survey for the week of October 26 printed $8.155, down 8.7 cents and inside our $8.14 to $8.26 range, against a national average of $6.352, up 7.6 cents. Gasoline went the way we said. Regular finished at $5.9055, down 11.8 cents and inside our $5.88 to $6.02 range; EIA regular printed $5.888, inside our $5.80 to $5.93. Wholesale reversed. The Los Angeles ultra-low-sulfur diesel spot, which our lean-higher call rested on, ran $5.390 on October 22, $5.299 on October 23, $5.190 on October 26 and $5.054 on October 27, down 31 cents from the October 20 print. Crude held up. WTI settled Friday at $104.12, up about 1% on the week and 15.2% in October, inside our $96 to $112 range, and Brent’s January contract at $113.43 (the December contract expired at $117.64). Our read: the gap we leaned on closed from the wholesale side, not the pump side. Los Angeles diesel is now within 4.3 cents of where it stood the week ending October 9, and the statewide pump has come down to meet it. Europe’s 42.6 million barrel reserve release is a cushion, and crude near $104 to $113 with Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia still running is the risk. We call diesel flat to slightly lower, and gasoline lower.

Well & Pump Efficiency

The shakers are parked and the wells are about to go quiet. That is the cheapest week of the year to find out which pumps are bleeding you. A test now means the bowl work, impeller trimming or machine work gets done in the off-season while nobody is waiting on water, and you walk into next season with a tested number to budget the irrigation power line against. At $8.46 diesel, a worn diesel-driven pump costs about 1.8 times what the identical wear costs on the meter. Here is what that is worth.

Electric at 35¢/kWh blended. Diesel at Fresno’s $8.457 pump (AAA, 11/1); off-road dyed runs about 72.6¢ lower, near $7.73. Per-acre figures assume 3.5 acre-feet on mature almonds. “Loss” is the extra cost of running a plant at 50% efficiency instead of a healthy 65%. Published guidance says 49% or less means retrofit, repair or adjustment. Pull your own tariff.

What that is on a real block. A 700-foot diesel well serving 40 acres of mature almonds at 50% efficiency is burning about $28,900 a season in pure waste, money that buys you no water and no crop. The same well on the meter wastes $16,200. A 400-foot diesel plant on 40 acres is $16,500. The 1.8-to-1 ratio holds at every depth, so if you can only afford to test a few pumps, test the diesel units first.

Why you test before you spend. A rising power bill does not tell you whether the pump is worn or the water simply got deeper. In SGMA country those are different problems with different fixes, a bowl job versus a deeper bore or a lower pump setting, and a test is the only thing that separates them. Money spent on the wrong one is gone. The tester takes four readings: standing and pumping water level (the difference is your drawdown), flow rate and discharge pressure, and power input, kWh draw for electric or fuel rate for diesel. Those give total dynamic head and overall plant efficiency. Turbine pumps are often just out of adjustment, and impeller clearance can sometimes be reset on the spot for immediate savings. A badly worn or corroded impeller cannot be adjusted back; that is machine work or replacement.
Who pays for the test. The Advanced Pumping Efficiency Program (APEP) is funded through the Public Purpose Programs charge you already pay on your PG&E bill and administered by the Center for Irrigation Technology at Fresno State. 800.845.6038. The subsidy goes to the testing company: $200 for a pump not tested in the prior 47 months, $100 if not tested in 23 months, $50 for a pump in series with another. You need a non-residential PG&E electric or gas account used for irrigation, 25 nameplate horsepower or more, paying the Public Purpose charge. One subsidized test per pump per 23 months. The subsidy may not cover the whole test, so get the total price in writing before you schedule. APEP also runs a separate diesel pumping efficiency track, the one to ask for at these prices. Retrofit cash incentives exist for bowl or impeller replacement, machine work and impeller trimming, but they require a pump test before and after, no more than three years apart. A VFD or motor swap by itself does not qualify.

Also live, and worth moving on: the state’s WETA program funds no-cost irrigation evaluations and pump efficiency tests through technical-assistance providers, and many of those grants end in 2026. With about eight weeks left in the year, call a provider this week rather than in January. CDFA lists the funded providers at cdfa.ca.gov/oars/technical/weta.html.

The Houthi and Hormuz reports come from wire services and military-monitor accounts; several claims remain unverified.

Refining · Stocks

Distillate Stocks Drew More Than Expected and Sit 10% Below Normal

EIA’s weekly table for the week ending October 23 put US distillate inventories at 102.8 million barrels, down 1.5 million when analysts expected a 0.9 million draw, and about 10% below the five-year average. Commercial crude stocks fell 1.9 million barrels to 410.6 million, the fourth straight weekly decline, gasoline stocks built 3.9 million to 209.2 million, and refinery utilization slipped 0.6 points to 92.8%. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve stands at 282.6 million barrels, down 30.9% from a year ago. Why it matters on the ground: thin diesel stocks are why we do not call a big drop even with Los Angeles wholesale lower. Gasoline is the other story, a 3.9 million barrel build is why we lean lower there.

Europe · Reserves

EU Will Release 42.6 Million Barrels From Strategic Stocks, Nearly Half of It Diesel

EU energy ministers meeting in Luxembourg on Thursday, October 29 agreed to speed the release of 42.6 million barrels of strategic reserves over the coming months, and Commissioner Dan Jorgensen said nearly half will be diesel. The Los Angeles diesel spot fell from $5.390 on October 22 to $5.054 on October 27, though we cannot tie that move to this announcement alone. For you: this is a cushion for the global diesel market California prices off, not new barrels landing in the Valley. It is a reason we lean flat to lower instead of higher, and it does nothing about the Houthi risk to crude.

Policy · Relief

USDA Harvest Fuel Relief: Title I Rates Due in November, Specialty Crop Growers Asked for Fuel Data

USDA’s $8 billion Harvest Fuel Relief program, administered by the Farm Service Agency, will pay a uniform per-acre formula based on modeled 2026 costs, not your fuel receipts. USDA says per-acre rates for Title I commodities will be released in November, and no sign-up date has been announced. Payment methods for other commodities will come later. Specialty crop growers, which is most of the Valley, are asked to send fuel cost data to HarvestFuel@usda.gov to help set their rates, and producers can set up a Login.gov account now to get a pre-filled application. California Citrus Mutual says it is working to get cost-per-acre estimates for California growers. What it means for your costs: nothing changes at the rack, and we would still not budget against money with no published rate. Emailing your fuel numbers costs you nothing and may shape the rate.

California · Supply

Two Closed Refineries Added Little to the Premium; the Spike Was Global, Berkeley Economist Argues

A UC Berkeley Energy Institute blog post of October 26 notes that Phillips 66 stopped running crude at its Los Angeles refinery in October 2025 and Valero began shutting down crude units at Benicia at the end of January 2026, together about 18% of California’s refining capacity. The author estimates the closures added only about 4 cents a gallon to the Los Angeles gasoline premium over the Gulf Coast, says the 2026 spike was largely global because the Gulf Coast refining margin rose almost as much, and notes that renewable diesel now supplies most of California’s in-state diesel. Why it matters on the ground: the California-over-national gap of $1.80 a gallon is a structural cost you carry in good weeks and bad. It narrowed this week because California fell while the national number rose, not because the premium went away. The swings you feel come from global crude and diesel margins, so that is where we keep our eyes.

What To Do This Week

✓ Action Items

1. Buy diesel as you use it, and do not chase. Wholesale gave back its jump and the pump has caught down to it. Do not sign a term price above this week’s level, and do not run tanks low waiting for a drop. The risk is a headline jump in crude, not a slow drift.
