Section 1
On the Governor's Desk
Decision due September 30
AB 2646 — a $19.75 hourly floor for ag
The bill sets a $19.75 per hour minimum for “approved agricultural employees” — H-2A and out-of-state seasonal workers — and for “corresponding employees,” meaning California residents doing comparable work for the same employer in the same county. The rate would index annually to the Social Security COLA.
It cleared the Assembly 58–16 and the Senate 30–8, and was presented to the Governor on August 31. He has until September 30 to sign or veto. If signed, it takes effect January 1, 2027.
What it means if you run any H-2A crew: the $19.75 would not stop at that crew. It would reach your domestic workers doing comparable work in the same county. Against the $17.40 statewide floor arriving January 1, that is about 13 percent on top — on every hour of comparable work, not just the visa hours.
Status as of press time. If you are budgeting 2027 labor, build both cases — signed and vetoed — before the end of the month.
Also awaiting a signature
AB 2227 — FLC bonds double. Farm labor contractor surety bonds go from $25,000 / $50,000 / $75,000 to $50,000 / $100,000 / $150,000, by payroll tier. Presented September 8. If you use a contractor, this lands on their renewal cost, and it will find its way into your rate.
AB 187 — sheepherder and goatherder wage parity extended to January 1, 2029. Presented September 8.
AB 2321 — expanded Cal/OSHA investigation authority and added criminal penalties. Effective January 1, 2027 if signed.
Farm automation authorization failed. The late budget-trailer language that would have opened the door to autonomous tractor operation died on labor opposition. It will be back.

The Department of Finance certified the 2027 rate on July 31 — a 2.99 percent CPI increase, just under the 3.5 percent statutory cap. The overtime phase-in is finished: since January 1, 2025 every California ag employer, regardless of headcount, owes overtime after 8 hours a day and 40 in a week, double time past 12, and the seventh-day premium on top. Small-employer relief is gone.
The January 1 payroll reset
•  Base hourly rate to $17.40
•  Piece-rate rest and recovery floor, and other nonproductive time, to $17.40
•  Exempt supervisor salaries to $72,384 a year — a crew boss below that, or paid hourly, is non-exempt and owed ag overtime
•  Reporting-time and split-shift premiums recalculated
•  Wage order postings and Labor Code 2810.5 new-hire notices reprinted
•  Written pay-change notices issued
Worth a second look at piece rate. Rest and recovery periods have to be paid separately, at the higher of your workweek average hourly rate or minimum wage, and other nonproductive time — waiting, moving between blocks, equipment moves, meetings — at no less than minimum wage. Both have to show on the stub as separate line items with hours, rate and gross. Wage statement penalties run $50 for the first pay period and $100 per employee per period after, and the Labor Commissioner recovered more than $6 million for farmworkers this year on sick leave and wage protections.
Section 3
The H-2A Desk
Preserve your records now
A court threw out the wage rule but left the rates standing
On August 26 a federal court in the Eastern District of California held that the Department of Labor's 2025 AEWR methodology was arbitrary and capricious — the two-tier skill structure, the housing adjustment, the use of a wage survey that excludes farms, and the way multi-duty jobs get classified. The court did not vacate it. Current rates stay enforceable while DOL builds a new method.
Here is the part that reaches your checkbook. DOL's own notice, posted September 2, says employers “may be required to make wage adjustment payments” covering the period from September 2 forward — to H-2A workers and to U.S. workers in corresponding employment. DOL says no obligation exists at this moment. That is not the same as saying none is coming.
Keep, starting today: exact hours and earnings by worker, occupational classification and actual job duties, and current contact information — name, address, SSN, I-94, email and phone — for every H-2A worker and every domestic worker in corresponding employment.

California certified 35,138 H-2A positions in FY2025, third in the country — but the state's share has slipped three years running while national use climbs. Nationally, first-half FY2026 certifications ran nearly 17 percent ahead of the year before. Remember the program is not just the wage: free housing, inbound and outbound travel with subsistence, daily transport, workers' compensation, the three-fourths guarantee, and filing fees that have risen sharply. Published work puts the non-wage load at roughly half again on top of the wage bill.
Section 4
Cal/OSHA Corner
Comment closes September 21
Cal/OSHA put out a revised heat illness draft on August 14 that would merge the indoor and outdoor rules. Three changes matter to you: the 95°F high-heat procedures would stop being limited to agriculture, construction, landscaping, oil and gas, and heavy transport — every outdoor employer would carry them; unacclimatized-worker procedures would extend to anyone returning after more than 14 days away; and the plan would have to be handed out at hire, at training, and annually. Rhabdomyolysis gets added to the definition of heat illness. It is a discussion draft, not a rule — but the comment window is open right now.

Both need a written plan in English and in the language most of your crew speaks, kept at the worksite. In 2025 the outdoor heat standard was the second most-cited Cal/OSHA standard in the state, behind only the Injury and Illness Prevention Program. Late reporting of a serious injury sat at number four — that one carries a $5,000 minimum on its own. A serious violation runs up to $25,000, and a willful one up to $162,851.
One more on the calendar: the Standards Board has to adopt a permanent workplace violence prevention standard by December 31. The SB 553 obligations you have carried since July 2024 — written plan, violent incident log, annual training — become a citable Title 8 section. Cal/OSHA publishes an agriculture-specific fact sheet on it, and it reaches employer-provided housing.

The wage number comes with an asterisk this year. That $20.35 California field-worker rate is from the April 2025 reference week, and it is the last one there will ever be — USDA discontinued the Farm Labor Survey in August 2025. The survey that set the AEWR for decades is gone, which is a large part of why the wage rule is now in front of a judge.
Workers' compensation is going up. The advisory pure premium benchmark rose to $1.65 per $100 of payroll effective September 1, a 6.6 percent increase. The rating bureau asked for 10.4 percent and the Insurance Commissioner adopted the lower figure — if you saw 10.4 in a trade headline, that was the request, not the result. Cumulative trauma claim frequency and medical costs are driving it. Review your experience modification before renewal.
Availability. A 2026 survey of specialty crop producers found 48 percent hit a labor shortage during the 2025 season, 41 percent used H-2A, and 96 percent saw labor costs rise or hold flat. About half either have automation in place or plan it within five years, with harvest at the top of the list.
On enforcement and absence. A survey of more than 500 California farmers across 50 counties, published in February, found fewer than 1 percent lost workers to a direct enforcement action on their own operation, while about 15 percent lost workers because of concerns about enforcement activity, and roughly 14 percent reduced production as a result. The documented disruption was people staying home, not raids on the ranch — and it landed hardest on labor-intensive crops.
Organizing. Eight California farms were certified through majority sign-up between October 2023 and November 2025, and there were no secret-ballot elections at all in 2023 or 2024 — a first in the Board's history. The Fifth District Court of Appeal dismissed Wonderful Nurseries' challenge to its certification in November 2025. A separate federal case brought by workers is still pending.

This Month in American History

September 17, 1787 — The Constitution Is Signed

Two hundred thirty-nine years ago today, in a Philadelphia room with the windows shut against eavesdroppers through a brutal summer, thirty-nine of the fifty-five delegates signed the Constitution. Three who stayed to the last day refused to sign it. Almost nobody in that room got everything he came for.

What they produced was not a finished country. It was a set of rules for arguing about one, written on purpose so it could be changed — and it has been changed twenty-seven times since. Franklin, eighty-one years old and carried to the hall each day because he could no longer walk it, signed anyway, and told the room that the older he got the more he distrusted his own judgment.

There is something fitting about running that anniversary in a report about regulation. The rules change. They are supposed to. The work is keeping up with them and staying in business while you do — and out here that has been the job for a long time.

We the People of the United States, in Order to form a more perfect Union, establish Justice … and secure the Blessings of Liberty to ourselves and our Posterity, do ordain and establish this Constitution.
