Section 1

Crop Status & Harvest Outlook

August is when the Valley starts cashing in the year. Almond shaking is running roughly a week and a half to two weeks ahead of normal, silage corn is being chopped, cotton is closing in on cutout, pomegranates are coloring up ahead of a fall pick, and pistachios are heading into a harvest that will be lighter than anyone hoped. The crop in front of you is largely set — but next year’s crop is decided by what you do on this ground in the next ninety days. That starts with a post-harvest soil sample.

🍐  Almonds Harvest Underway
Shaking has started on Nonpareil and early varieties — roughly 1.5 to 2 weeks ahead of normal. USDA pegs the 2026 crop at 2.70 billion lbs, down 1% from last year, and handler estimates have since drifted to 2.65–2.70 billion on a warm spring that pushed maturity and trimmed kernel weights. Bearing acreage fell for the first time in 30 years. Keep NOW sanitation tight, get nuts off the ground fast, and pull soil samples right behind the sweeper.
🌿  Pistachios Short Off-Year Crop
An off year compounded by an unusually hot March. Pollen viability drops above roughly 85°F during bloom, and orchards — especially south of Fresno County — are showing high blank counts. Early estimates run 600–900 million lbs against last year’s record 1.6 billion. Harvest approaches late month into September. Industry leaders call 2026 an anomaly and expect a strong 2027. Use this light year to fix the soil.
🪓  Walnuts Kernel Fill / Pre-Harvest
Kernels filling and packing tissue browning — early harvest is four to six weeks out. Walnut husk fly and third-flight codling moth remain the pest calls. Hold water steady through fill, then plan your pre-harvest dry-down. Sunburn is the quiet yield thief in a hot August — keep canopies covered and irrigation ahead of the heat.
🍇  Wine & Table Grapes Harvest / Post-Veraison
San Joaquin table grapes are in full harvest; early wine varieties are coming off late month. Nitrogen should be done — finish on potassium and water management to hit sugar and color targets. Shade clusters through heat spikes. Book post-harvest sampling now so the fall amendment pass is planned before the crews are gone.
🥝  Kiwis Final Sizing
Fruit is putting on its last size ahead of an October harvest. Vines are shallow-rooted and unforgiving in triple digits — keep water heavy and consistent. Maintain potassium for size and dry matter. Shade the fruit; sunburn losses this month are permanent. Watch mites and leafroller.
🍊  Citrus Valencia Harvest / Sizing
Valencias are moving while new-crop navels size for fall. Steady moisture prevents splitting and drop — August swings cost fruit. Keep potassium available. Citrus thrips and red scale pressure continues, and Asian Citrus Psyllid monitoring stays a top priority — report symptoms to CDFA. Protect young trees from heat.
🍀  Pomegranates Sizing & Color Development
Wonderful is putting on final size and building aril color ahead of a late-September through November pick. Two things decide this crop in August. First, irrigation consistency — pomegranates split when soil moisture swings, and a split fruit is a total loss, not a downgrade. Hold a steady moisture band instead of chasing the heat. Second, leaffooted bug, which UC IPM flags as building to very large numbers from mid-August through harvest. Late-season feeding often leaves no mark on the outside while the arils darken and wither underneath — and the wound opens a door for fungal entry. Get scouting in place now. Shade exposed fruit as well; sunburn on the sun side is permanent. Keep potassium up for size and color, with calcium supporting rind integrity, and watch Alternaria heart rot in any block carrying late moisture.
🌿  Cotton Boll Fill / Cutout
Bolls are filling and fields are moving toward cutout. Nitrogen should be finished — late N only delays maturity. Keep water on until the last harvestable boll is set, then start planning termination. Watch lygus, aphids, and mites through cutout; sticky cotton from late aphids is a grading problem you cannot fix at the gin.
🌽  Corn (Silage & Grain) Silage Chopping
Early silage is being chopped; later plantings are in grain fill. Chop to moisture and kernel milk line, not the calendar — it drives feed value all winter. Dairy demand stays firm. Sample chopped ground immediately so double-crop or fall cover decisions are made on data, not habit.
🌾  Wheat Stubble & Open Ground Sampling Window Open
Wheat ground is open and orchards are opening up behind harvest — this is the sampling window. A post-harvest soil test shows what the crop actually pulled out, where salts and pH have moved, and which nutrients are sitting there tied up and unavailable. That data is what turns a fall amendment and custom-blend program into a targeted plan instead of a guess. Sample now; the window closes when the ground gets wet.

Section 2

August Fertilizer Requirements

August fertility splits in two directions. On crops still filling, potassium finishes the job while nitrogen stays off. On crops coming off, the priority shifts to post-harvest recovery — the single highest-return fertility window in tree crops. Post-harvest zinc and boron set next year’s bud, and a post-harvest soil test tells you what your custom blend needs to carry. Apply what the ground asks for, and nothing it doesn’t.

Crop Primary Nutrients Micros / Notes Timing & Method
Almonds In-season N and K are done. Post-harvest is the window — modest N and K restore reserves before leaf drop. Post-harvest Zn & B foliar sets next year’s bud. HIGH Soil sample behind the sweeper. Irrigate up after harvest, then fertigate. Foliar Zn/B before leaf drop.
Pistachios Finish K into harvest. Cut N to match the light crop — a short crop does not need a full-crop N budget. Post-harvest B is critical for 2027 bloom. HIGH Address salinity & pH now. Soil test post-harvest, then build a corrective blend for fall.
Walnuts K through kernel fill. No further N — late N delays hull split and hurts color. Zn & Mn foliar if tissue is short. MED Plan post-harvest program. Fertigate K; taper water into pre-harvest dry-down.
Grapes Nitrogen off. Potassium only, to finish sugar and color. Nothing heavy near pick. Post-harvest N & Zn restore reserves. MED Respect PHI on everything. Fertigate to quality targets; resume feeding after the last pick.
Kiwis Potassium for final sizing and dry matter. Hold N — soft growth invites damage. Ca supports storage life. MED Keep micros balanced. Fertigate every 10–14 days. Water stays heavy and steady.
Citrus N: 15–25 lbs/ac on sizing blocks. K for size and split prevention. Zn & Mn foliar. MED Iron chelate if chlorotic. Fertigate every 7–14 days with steady summer irrigation.
Pomegranates K drives size and aril color. Taper N now — late nitrogen delays color and softens rind. Ca supports rind integrity & split resistance. HIGH B & Zn per tissue. Fertigate K on a steady schedule. Even moisture matters more than rate.
Cotton Nitrogen complete. Potassium through boll fill only — heavy loads pull K hard late. Foliar K on heavy sets. HIGH Late N delays cutout. Foliar or fertigated K to cutout, then shut it down.
Corn N is finished on silage ground. Grain corn: K per soil test through fill. Sample chopped ground for residual N. HIGH Credit manure history. Test immediately after chopping to size the next crop’s program.
Open Ground No in-season fertility. Build the fall program from a fresh post-harvest test. Check pH, salinity, OM & base saturation. LOW Amend before rain. Sample now; spread a custom corrective blend ahead of winter moisture.
Advisor Note: Post-harvest is when a tree decides next year’s crop. Trees that go into dormancy with full carbohydrate and nutrient reserves set stronger buds and bloom more evenly — which matters enormously in a pistachio block coming off a short year and heading into an expected on-year. Sample first, then blend. Applying a full-rate maintenance program to ground that already has the nutrient sitting there is money spent twice.

This Month in American History

August 2, 1776 — The Day They Actually Signed It

Independence was declared on July 4, 1776 — but the parchment most Americans picture was not signed that day. On August 2, 1776, at the Pennsylvania State House in Philadelphia, the delegates of the Second Continental Congress gathered to put their names to the engrossed copy of the Declaration of Independence. John Hancock signed first, bold and unmistakable, at the top.

Fifty-six men eventually signed, arranged by state from New Hampshire down to Georgia. Some were not in Philadelphia that August day and added their names weeks later. Every one of them knew what a signature meant: under British law it was treason, punishable by death. They signed anyway.

Two hundred fifty years later, that’s the part worth remembering. July 4 was the decision. August 2 was the commitment — the day ordinary men put their own names on the line and made it permanent. Farmers understand that difference. Anyone can plan a crop in the winter; it’s signing for the ground and the water and the inputs that makes you responsible for it.
