Time-Sensitive — Get Sampled Before the Rain

A wet winter is coming. Your sampling window just got shorter.

NOAA has an El Niño Advisory in effect and puts the odds of a very strong event this fall and winter at better than 90%. If that verifies, the dry stretch between harvest and the first real storms is the only clean shot we get.

That matters because this is the sample that tells you what this year's crop actually pulled out of the ground — and that number is what a targeted nutrition plan is built from. 2027 is an on-year for pistachios, and the almonds and the rest of the permanent crops are set up for a good one. Feed them off data, not off habit.

Section 1

Crop Status & Harvest

September is the busiest month on the calendar and this year it started early. First almond shakers went into western Colusa County ground on July 10, and a late-July heat wave that ran 102–105°F pulled everything forward and compressed the window. The pistachio crop is the story of the year — and not a good one. Meanwhile the ground is opening up behind harvest, and that is where next year gets decided.

🍐 Almonds

Harvest Underway
Running two to three weeks ahead of normal. Nonpareil is off in most districts and pollinators are coming behind fast — expect a compressed run. Watch thick hulls slowing huller flow rates. Red blotch is the new problem: it went from one Merced County orchard in 2024 to most of the Valley by 2025, and untreated blocks are defoliating. Keep navel orangeworm sanitation tight and get mummies knocked this winter.

🌿 Pistachios

Short Crop — Blanks
This one hurts. Heat during bloom pushed past roughly 85°F and cooked pollen viability, and the southern San Joaquin took the worst of it. Blank shells are showing up everywhere. Industry estimates put the crop at 600–900 million pounds against a record 1.5 billion+ in 2025. Harvest what you have, keep the trees alive, and start planning a strong dormant and post-harvest program for 2027.

🪓 Walnuts

Harvest Begins
Packing tissue browning is the trigger — check it, do not guess. Early varieties start mid-September. Ethephon timing is the single biggest quality call you make. Keep water on until shake to protect kernel color, then cut cleanly. Husk fly damage shows up now — anything stained is a grade problem. Get hulled and dried fast; heat plus delay equals dark kernels.

🍇 Wine & Table Grapes

Crush Underway
Whites are in, reds are coming. The hot summer moved sugars fast — sample by the block, not by the ranch, or you will pick unevenly. Watch for sunburn and raisining on west-facing fruit. Any late-season moisture makes sour rot a real threat on tight clusters. Table grape quality is good; pack out clean and move it.

🥝 Kiwis

Final Sizing
Last sizing push before an October harvest. Do not shut water off early — kiwi puts on measurable size in the final four weeks. Start dry matter and soluble solids testing mid-month so the pick date is a number, not a hunch. Keep the canopy shaded over exposed fruit; September sun still burns.

🍊 Citrus

Sizing / Pre-Color
Navels are putting on their last size before color break. Hold water steady — swings now cause splitting, and a hot September makes it worse. Late Valencias still hanging need protection from sunburn. Keep Asian citrus psyllid monitoring current and report anything suspicious to CDFA. Red scale and citrus leafminer are both active.

🍀 Pomegranates

Harvest Begins
Wonderful harvest opens late September into October. Cracking is the whole game — keep soil moisture even and resist the urge to push a big late irrigation. Sunburn on exposed shoulders is heavy after this summer. Pick by internal color and acid, not by calendar. Sort hard at the shed; one split fruit contaminates a bin.

🌿 Cotton

Boll Opening / Defoliation
Cut-out is behind us; bolls are cracking. Terminate irrigation on the last effective boll population — water past that point only costs money and delays defoliation. Target defoliation at 60–70% open boll or four nodes above cracked boll. Warm nights are working in your favor for harvest-aid activity. Watch late whitefly for sticky cotton.

🌽 Corn (Silage & Grain)

Silage Chopping
Silage chopping is running hard across the dairy belt. Chop at 32–38% dry matter — the heat is drying fields faster than the calendar suggests, so test, do not estimate. Pack density and a fast seal are worth more than anything else you do this month. Grain corn is drying down toward an October cut. Second-crop and double-crop ground needs a soil test before it gets planted again.

🌾 Wheat & Open Ground

Plan Now — Plant Nov
This is the month open ground earns its keep. Fallow and post-silage fields are the easiest, cleanest sampling you will ever pull — no canopy, no traffic, no fertigation residue. Get pH, salinity, sodium, phosphorus and organic matter on paper now so the fall amendment plan and the November wheat program are built on data instead of last year's habit.

Section 2

September Fertilizer Program

Post-harvest is the most misunderstood window of the year. The trees are still photosynthesizing and roots are still active, so what goes on now is stored and spent on next spring's bloom and leaf-out. But not everything belongs here. Late-fall nitrogen has not increased yield in well-managed orchards carrying adequate summer leaf N — that is money you can keep. Boron, zinc and banded potassium are where the return actually is.

Crop Primary Nutrients Micros & Notes Timing & Method
Almonds Band dry K post-harvest at 200–250 lbs K₂O/ac where CEC supports it — banks the reservoir and frees spring calendar space. Skip late-fall N in blocks with adequate summer leaf N. Fall foliar B at 0.2–0.4 lb B/ac (1–2 lb Solubor per acre in 100 gal) where hull B is low to adequate. Zn at 5 lb/ac zinc sulfate in October beats 20+ lb in November. HIGH Foliars to full canopy before defoliation. Do not tank-mix Zn and B without dropping pH to about 5.0.
Pistachios Light crop means the tree kept its carbohydrate. Do not overfeed N chasing last year's numbers. Modest post-harvest K and P to rebuild reserves. Post-harvest Zn and B foliar for next year's bloom. MED Pull leaf samples now — a short year is the best diagnostic year you get. Keep irrigation on after shake. Foliar before leaf drop.
Walnuts Hold N until after harvest, then a light post-harvest application only where leaf N came back low. K by soil test. Zn foliar post-harvest on deficient blocks. MED Watch B — walnuts are easy to push into toxicity. After shake, while leaves are still green and functional.
Grapes Nothing before the pick. Post-harvest N and K restore reserves for next year's shoots and set. Post-harvest Zn and B foliar. MED Petiole results from veraison should drive the rate. Two to four weeks after harvest while the canopy is still working.
Kiwis Taper N now. Keep K moving through final sizing — it drives dry matter and storage life. Ca supports firmness at harvest. MED Excess late N softens fruit and shortens storage. Fertigation every 10–14 days until the last two weeks pre-harvest.
Citrus Final N of the season goes on now — later applications delay color break and hurt rind quality. K supports size and rind strength. Zn and Mn foliar on the fall flush. MED Steady moisture matters more than any nutrient for split control. Fertigation, then stop N. Foliar micros on hardened fall flush.
Pomegranates No N into harvest. Post-harvest K and a modest N application rebuild reserves once the fruit is off. Ca through sizing helps rind integrity. HIGH Even moisture is the single biggest cracking control. Hold steady irrigation through pick, then fertigate post-harvest.
Cotton Fertility season is closed. Any additional N now delays cut-out, regrows the plant, and fights your harvest aid. No foliar micros. LOW Post-harvest soil test drives next year's plan. Terminate irrigation on last effective boll. Sample after defoliation.
Corn No further N. After chopping, credit the manure and lagoon water already applied before you write a single pound into next year's plan. Post-harvest residual nitrate is the number that matters. HIGH Dairies: this feeds your INMP reporting too. Sample after chop, before the next crop or amendment goes on.
Wheat / Open Ground Nothing goes on until the soil test comes back. Build the P and K correction and the starter plan off September numbers. Gypsum, lime and sulfur decisions all get made from this test. HIGH Apply amendments ahead of winter rain so they have time to react. Sample September, amend October, plant November.
Input Cost Note: We are not going to quote you a price — what you actually pay swings hard by product, volume, delivery point and who you buy from, and a number in a newsletter is worth less than a real quote. But the direction is worth knowing. Phosphate has been climbing all year, and the reason is sulfur: DAP takes roughly four-tenths of a ton of sulfur to make, sulfur supply tightened after Middle East disruptions and export restrictions out of China and Russia, and that cost went straight into phosphate. Ammonia has moved up alongside it. Nitrogen has been the softer corner of the market by comparison. What that means for you is simple — if a September soil test turns up a phosphorus correction, get it quoted early rather than waiting for the spring rush, and let the test tell you whether you need it at all.

Lead Commodity — September

Pistachios: a short crop the market cannot fully price back

600–900 million lb industry estimates • vs. 1.5 billion+ in 2025

A hot bloom did this. Once temperatures push past roughly 85°F during flowering, pistachio pollen loses much of its viability — and the southern San Joaquin Valley got exactly that. The result is blanks: shells that never filled. Alternate bearing was always going to pull 2026 down from a record 2025, but heat turned a down year into a short year.

Price will not make growers whole. As one Valley grower put it plainly, when you budget a 2 billion pound crop and harvest closer to 800 million, the price cannot climb far enough to cover the gap without pushing buyers away. Handlers are protecting long-term customers rather than chasing a spike, and tighter Iranian supply is firming the global picture without solving the local one.

What we would do: resist the temptation to strip inputs to the bone. Cutting water and fertilizer to survive this year is exactly how a short year becomes two short years. Pull leaf and soil samples this fall, spend where the return is real — post-harvest zinc and boron, and correcting whatever the soil test actually shows — and go into 2027 with a healthy tree instead of a starved one.

Spotlight: Soil & Water Testing

This month the spotlight is not on a product. It is on the two tests that everything else is built from — and on the paperwork trail behind them. Post-harvest is the cleanest sampling window of the year: no canopy in the way, no fertigation residue skewing the numbers, and ground open enough to actually get across.

Already Awarded a Soil Grant?

We handle the sampling, the lab work, and the amendment sourcing.

Getting selected is the good news. Then comes the part nobody budgets time for: pulling the baseline samples to protocol, getting results back from a lab the program will accept, and sourcing compost and amendments from certified providers that meet the program's specifications — with documentation that holds up.

We do all of it. We pull the cores on the right schedule and to the right depth, run the analysis through recognized methods, hand you results you can file, and then tell you which certified compost and amendment sources actually match what your award calls for and what your soil report says you need. Two different questions — and you have to satisfy both.

The timing rule that trips people up: the baseline sample has to be pulled before the practice goes in the ground. Amend first and you have no defensible “before” number — which means either a delayed practice or a compliance problem at reporting. Call us before the spreader rolls, not after.

This Month in American History

September 11, 2001 — Twenty-Five Years

Twenty-five years ago this month, an ordinary Tuesday morning turned into the day that changed the country. 2,977 people were killed at the World Trade Center, at the Pentagon, and in a field outside Shanksville, Pennsylvania. Among them were 343 New York firefighters, 37 Port Authority police officers, and 23 NYPD officers — men and women who ran up the stairs while everyone else was running down.

Out here, the news came in over the radio in the cab of a tractor, or on a kitchen TV before dawn, three hours behind New York. The work still had to get done that day. It always does. But something in the country shifted, and a generation of young men and women from towns like ours signed up because of what they saw that morning.

A quarter century on, there are people driving trucks and running crews in this Valley who were not born yet. That is exactly why we keep telling it. Twenty-five years is long enough for a day to become history — and short enough that we still owe it to remember out loud.
