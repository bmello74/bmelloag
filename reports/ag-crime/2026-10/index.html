Walk Your Orchards This Week

Fresno County detectives believe more stolen harvest equipment may still be sitting in orchards. Thieves dumped tractors, almond conditioners and harvesters in rural orchards around Kerman to cool off before moving them. If you see iron on your ground that is not yours, do not move it. Section 1

Statewide Snapshot

October is when the harvest iron sits still. Almonds are done or nearly done, pistachios and walnuts are finishing, and conditioners, harvesters and shakers that ran sixteen hours a day in September are parked at the end of a block or in a yard nobody is watching anymore. That is exactly what thieves went after this month. The two biggest cases of the month were not copper. They were a million dollars of harvest equipment and a $400,000 load of almonds, and both left on wheels.

October Headlines: The Harvest Left On Wheels

Fresno County Sep 24–28

$1 million in harvest equipment found from the air

The Sheriff’s Air Support Unit flew Fresno County farm ground looking for stolen equipment, and spotted possible matches in rural Kerman. Ag Task Force detectives checked each site on the ground. Over three recoveries (near Mendota and Firebaugh, northwest of Kerman, and in an orchard southwest of Kerman) they found three John Deere tractors, two Flory almond conditioners, a Flory almond harvester and two Jack Rabbit harvesters, valued together at roughly $1.07 million. Everything went back to its owners. No arrests have been announced.

Why it matters: this was mid-harvest equipment, some of it 2026 models, taken and parked in other people’s orchards. Detectives think there is more out there.
Kerman → San Joaquin Oct 2

A $400,000 almond load, recovered two counties away

Kerman Police took a report of a stolen cargo load of almonds valued at about $400,000. Working with the CHP’s Delta Regional Auto Theft Task Force and the San Joaquin County Sheriff’s Office, investigators traced the load to San Joaquin County, where assisting agencies made four arrests. The investigation is still open, and police have not said how the load was taken.

Why it matters: almonds move north on the freeway fast. A load that is not verified before it leaves the yard can be in another county by breakfast.
The through-line in both cases: the property was found because somebody could prove it was theirs and said so quickly. A detective standing next to a tractor in a stranger’s orchard needs a reported theft and a serial number to match it to. Equipment nobody reported is equipment nobody can give back. Fresno’s Ag Task Force is at (559) 600-8150; Tulare’s Ag Crimes Unit is at (559) 733-6218.

Treat every number in this report as a floor, not a ceiling. Investigators statewide say a large share of ag theft is never reported at all.
🚚  Vehicles & Equipment Trend #1
Back on top this month, and it was not ATVs. The Fresno recoveries were tractors and nut-harvest equipment: the machines that cost the most to replace mid-season and are hardest to rent on short notice. Some were 2026 models. Most were stolen in September and parked in orchards to see if anyone came looking. Thieves stage stolen equipment and wait, which is why a same-day report and a tracker that tells you where it went both matter more than anything else on this list.
🍇  The Crop Itself Trend #2
A $400,000 almond load and ten citrus trees bookend the category. The almond case is the volume end: a whole cargo load moved two counties north before it was recovered. The citrus case is the other end: ten trees, about $3,500, taken from a grower near North and Temperance Avenues and recovered on a search warrant in Sanger. With walnuts coming in, Sutter County has moved to fix its walnut buying period at November 1 through April 30 under its Walnut Theft Prevention Ordinance, a rule built around proving who owns the nuts.
⚡  Copper & Wire Rising
Not the headline this month, but not going anywhere. In late September the Yolo County Sheriff’s Office sought two Stockton residents on felony warrants for conspiracy and grand theft over copper wire stripped from farm irrigation pumps. Colusa County has asked the public for help with copper losses running into the hundreds of thousands, and notes many cold-season thefts are not found until spring. Post-harvest is the exposure: pumps go quiet and nobody walks the well for weeks.
⛽  Fuel & Chemicals Seasonal
No major new fuel or chemical case reached us for September, which we read as thin reporting, not a quiet month. The pattern from this summer still holds: a Merced County arrest over more than 500 gallons of red diesel taken from farms in Merced and Madera, and a Kern County search warrant in July that turned up stolen diesel alongside copper wire. Tanks that stop getting used after harvest are tanks nobody is metering.
Policy watch: a second metal theft law. On September 30, Governor Newsom signed AB 1941, which sets misdemeanor and felony penalties for people who repeatedly steal or traffic prohibited metal materials. It was written with streetlights and telecom lines in mind, but it builds on last year’s scrap documentation law, AB 476, and it is aimed squarely at the repeat crews and the buyers behind them. Most new California statutes take effect January 1. What it means for you: repeat-offender charges depend on cases being tied together, and that takes reports. Report every cut line, even the small ones.

Section 3

County Briefs

Region 1: Southern San Joaquin Valley

Fresno County Critical
More than $1 million in stolen harvest equipment recovered in five days. The Air Support Unit spotted the equipment from the air in late September and Ag Task Force detectives confirmed it on the ground: tractors, almond conditioners and harvesters, some stolen only the week before near Yuba and Shields Avenues. Detectives are still working to identify suspects and believe more may be abandoned in orchards. Separately, a Sanger man was charged with felony grand theft after ten citrus trees worth about $3,500 were recovered on a search warrant. Check your ground for equipment that isn’t yours.

Watch: parked harvest equipment, west-side orchards, tractors, nursery stock.
Tulare County Critical
No new Ag Crimes Unit release reached us after the two camera cases we covered last month. That is a reporting gap, not a quiet county. The pressure here is copper: in June, one South Tulare County farm lost pump wire twice in 48 hours, about $150 of copper and more than $20,000 in damage, with trees going without water. An Exeter pump company told reporters it handles theft-related repairs six to seven times a week. Reported losses topped $1 million between January and September 2025, and the sheriff’s office says many incidents never get reported at all.

Watch: well pumps after the water goes off, citrus blocks, staged nut trailers.
Kern County Critical
Copper off ag equipment has been the Rural Crimes Unit’s running case all year. In March, detectives arrested an unlicensed recycler with about 175 pounds of copper wire and records of more than 3,200 pounds recycled in two months; in April the sheriff flagged a significant increase in thefts in the Lamont, Arvin and Tejon areas; in July a search warrant near Old River turned up stolen farm diesel and copper. No new September release reached us. Crews scout first and come back when nobody is there.

Watch: remote wells, ATVs, diesel tanks, unlit equipment yards.
Kings County High
Copper remains the county’s problem, and the Rural Crime Unit says so. The unit reported more than $400,000 in copper losses during 2025, most of it off ag well pumps, and points at cordless reciprocating saws as the tool that made it a two-minute job. No new Kings case reached us for September. With harvest wrapping up and pumps going quiet, outlying wells are the exposure for the next several months.

Watch: well pumps on outlying ground, panel boxes, night access roads.

Region 2: Northern San Joaquin Valley

Madera County High
This is the county where growers stopped a copper theft in progress. In January, farmers interrupted a copper theft, blocked the suspects’ vehicle and held it until deputies arrived; three people were arrested. In June, Operation Round Up with Fresno County recovered roughly $150,000 in stolen ag chemicals. The Fresno recoveries this month were just across the line near Mendota and Firebaugh. West-side equipment yards are one corridor, not two counties.

Watch: parked harvesters, chemical storage, bulk fuel tanks.
Merced County High
Fuel is the local pressure point, and it accumulates quietly. The summer’s reference case was a Sheriff’s Office arrest over more than 500 gallons of red diesel taken from farms across Merced and Madera counties, taken in increments small enough that nobody caught it until somebody started counting. No new Merced ag case reached us for September. A weekly meter reading is still the entire countermeasure.

Watch: bulk fuel tanks, pump meters, dairy shops, idle harvest equipment.
Stanislaus County High
Walnut harvest keeps this a load-theft county through October. The month’s $400,000 almond load was taken in Fresno County and recovered in neighboring San Joaquin, on the same Highway 99 corridor that runs through Stanislaus. The exposure here is the yard and the road: loaded trailers staged overnight, huller and processor gates, and product waiting on a pickup. Verify every carrier against dispatch before a trailer leaves.

Watch: loaded trailers left overnight, huller yards, irrigation hardware.
San Joaquin County High
The stolen almond load ended up here, and so did four arrests. Sheriff’s deputies and the CHP’s Delta Regional Auto Theft Task Force recovered the Kerman load in the county in early October. Freeway access is why: product moves out fast. The Sheriff’s Office has also put drones to work on ag cases; one caught a fertilizer thief this spring. Anything loaded and left near highway access overnight is the exposure.

Watch: trailers, hitch locks, walnut loads, yard gates near highway access.

Region 3: Central Coast

Monterey County High
In June the Ag Crimes Unit closed a $50,000+ copper case off cameras and plate readers. Detectives used surveillance cameras and Flock license plate readers to identify a suspect in a series of copper wire thefts from ag properties in Monterey and surrounding counties; the wire had been sold to recyclers in the Central Valley. Earlier this year the unit broke an equipment rental-fraud ring and recovered about $300,000 in property, and closed a 1,000-gallon diesel case. This unit builds cases off recorded evidence.

Watch: pump wire, fuel tanks, verify every rental ID.
Ventura County High
The county runs Farm Watch, and it is free. It is a county-wide electronic neighborhood watch for farms, ranches, nurseries and supply houses, carrying current crime trends and guidance on preserving a scene until deputies arrive. The Sheriff’s Office asks for the same three things every time: pull the keys, secure the equipment, and put up cameras. Tractor and fruit theft on the Oxnard Plain and up the Santa Clara River remain the standing problem; no new ag case reached us for September.

Watch: tractors left in blocks, avocado and citrus picks, tool trailers.
Santa Barbara & SLO Elevated
Wine grape harvest is winding down, and that changes the exposure. Bins, crew trailers and harvest equipment that were in daily use sit idle at the end of a row. No new ag case reached us for September.

Watch: drip and sprinkler hardware, bins, crew tool trailers.
San Benito & Santa Cruz Elevated
Smaller acreage, same crews. Crews working the Salinas Valley do not stop at a county line, and Monterey’s copper case reached into surrounding counties. No new local case reached us for September.

Watch: pump houses, drip line, portable generators.

Region 4: Sacramento Valley, Delta & North Bay

Yolo, Solano & Sacramento Elevated
Yolo County is seeking two Stockton residents in copper thefts from farm irrigation pumps. The Sheriff’s Office announced felony warrants for conspiracy and grand theft in late September and asked for the public’s help. Levee roads give quiet access and a quick exit, and walnut harvest keeps equipment and fuel staged in the open through October. A crew from out of the county is a crew that already knows where the pumps are.

Watch: irrigation pumps, panel wire, ATVs, levee-road access points.
Butte, Glenn, Colusa & Sutter Elevated
Walnut harvest and copper are the two stories here this fall. Colusa County has asked the public for help with copper losses in the hundreds of thousands of dollars, and warns cold-season thefts often go unnoticed until spring. Sutter County moved to fix its walnut buying period at November 1 through April 30 under its Walnut Theft Prevention Ordinance. Keep your proof of ownership with every walnut load.

Watch: pump panels, walnut loads, rice harvest equipment, fuel.
Napa & Sonoma Elevated
Crush is winding down, and post-crush is when gate codes should change. Forklifts, crush pad equipment and finished goods all sat in one place under time pressure through harvest, with contract crews and delivery traffic moving daily. Change codes and collect keys now that the crews are gone. No new ag case reached us for September.

Watch: crush pad equipment, forklifts, finished-goods storage.
Far North & Foothills Monitor
Livestock and hay, with long response times and thin patrol coverage. Fall shipping season is when a current brand matters most: a mark that cannot be pried off or explained away, backed by a county record and a peace officer’s authority to stop a load and read it. Check your registration before cattle move.

Watch: brand registration current, gates, hay stacks, remote water.

Region 5: Southern California & Desert

Imperial County Elevated
Irrigation hardware is the target of choice, and the county answers it with monitoring. This spring, after about 450 sprinkler heads were stripped from one McConnell Road property in two incidents, investigators set up monitoring on the site and caught a third theft in progress. No new Imperial ag case reached us for September. A repeat location is a solvable location if somebody is watching it.

Watch: sprinkler heads, gated pipe, valves, hay stacks.
Riverside, San Bernardino & San Diego Elevated
Urban edge is the whole problem. Groves and nursery yards backing onto residential and industrial ground give short approach distances and easy resale. On September 30, Los Angeles County deputies served a warrant on an unlicensed Compton dismantling yard suspected of buying stolen copper, a reminder that the outlet is often a short drive away. Perimeter and lighting do more here than anywhere else in the state.

Watch: perimeter gates, avocado and citrus picks, nursery yards.

Section 4

Prevention Playbook

Nobody secures a whole farm. The goal is narrower and achievable: make your operation slower and louder than the one down the road, and make sure that whatever does go missing can be found and proven yours. This month’s cases were about the second half of that sentence.

The Post-Harvest Checklist

Twelve things worth doing before the harvest equipment is parked for the winter

✓ Write down every serial number before the equipment is parked for the winter. Tractors, conditioners, harvesters, shakers, trailers, pumps. Photograph the plate. Fresno’s recovered equipment went home because it could be matched.
✓ Stamp an Owner-Applied Number. Investigators recommend registering an OAN so recovered equipment can be traced back to you even if the factory plate is gone.
✓ Bring harvest equipment into a fenced, lit yard. A conditioner left at the end of a block for three weeks is an invitation. If it can’t come in, block it in.
✓ Pull the keys. Every machine, every night. Many equipment brands share keys. A key in the ignition saves a thief the only hard part.
✓ Change factory passcodes and immobilizer PINs. In a 2024 multi-county equipment ring case, thieves arrived with a key and the factory passcode for a $150,000 backhoe.
✓ Report a theft the same day. Thieves park stolen equipment in orchards to see if anyone comes looking. The report is what lets a deputy who finds it call you.
✓ Walk your orchards for iron that isn’t yours. Don’t touch it or move it. Call your county ag or rural crime unit.
✓ Verify every load pickup against the scheduled carrier. Call the broker, check the driver’s ID against dispatch, and photograph the tractor and trailer numbers before the load rolls.
✓ Carry proof of ownership with walnut loads. Sutter and other walnut counties tie their theft ordinances to who owns the nuts.
✓ Walk your wells after the water goes off. Post-harvest is when a cut line goes unnoticed for weeks. Put it on somebody’s route.
✓ Lock and meter the fuel tank. A tank that stops getting used after harvest is a tank nobody is watching.
✓ Report it, then tell your neighbors. Crews work corridors, not single ranches. The call that saves the next place is the one you make tonight.
The latest in prevention is older than you think. Fresno found a million dollars of equipment this month by putting an aircraft over orchards. That is a good result, but you cannot count on a helicopter. A tracker on the machine tells you where it went the same morning. In a 2024 multi-county case, a tracking alert sent detectives to a stolen backhoe loaded on a semi at a Tulare truck stop, which unraveled a ring moving equipment out of the Valley. In the same case, a $150,000 backhoe was never recovered: the thieves had a key, knew the factory passcode, and pulled the tracker. A tracker works when it is hidden, backed up, and paired with the basics. That is what we cover in Section 5.

This Month in American History

October 4, 1957: The Beep That Became GPS

On October 4, 1957, the Soviet Union put Sputnik into orbit: a polished sphere about the size of a beach ball, doing nothing but sending a steady radio beep. Within days, two physicists at the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory noticed they could work out the satellite’s path just by listening to how that beep shifted in pitch as it passed overhead.

Their boss asked the obvious next question: if you can find a satellite from a known spot on the ground, can you find a spot on the ground from a known satellite? That idea became Transit, the Navy’s satellite navigation system, and Transit led to the GPS network that guides our sampling rigs, steers tractors down a row and, hidden under a seat, tells a farmer where his stolen machine spent the night.

It is a very American story: a rival’s achievement landed as a shock, and the answer was not panic but ingenuity. A handful of people listened carefully to a problem and built a tool the whole country now uses without thinking about it. That is the same spirit that runs a good farm, and a good investigation.
